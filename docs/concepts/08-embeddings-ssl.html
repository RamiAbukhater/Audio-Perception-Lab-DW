Embeddings, clustering, and self-supervised learning

Module 2 starts with no sound labels at all. To find structure in hours of unlabelled kitchen and office audio, we first turn each short window into an embedding, a vector where similar sounds land close together, and then look for groups. This page covers what makes a good embedding, how to learn one without labels, and how to cluster it without fooling yourself.

The big idea

A classifier maps a sound to one of a fixed set of labels. An encoder maps a sound to a point in a space of, say, 128 or 512 dimensions, with no labels involved. If the encoder is good, microwave beeps form one tight cloud, keyboard clatter another, and running water a third, even though nobody told it those words. Once the clouds exist, naming them takes very few labels, or, in Module 2, none: we borrow the names from what the camera sees at the same moment.

What an embedding is

Take any trained audio network and cut off its final classification layer. The vector going into that layer (after global pooling) is an embedding. In TinyAudioNet (Concept 5) that is the 128-dimensional output of self.pool(x).flatten(1).

class Encoder(torch.nn.Module):
    """Everything in TinyAudioNet except the classification head."""
    def __init__(self, net):
        super().__init__()
        self.stem, self.blocks, self.pool = net.stem, net.blocks, net.pool
    def forward(self, x):                            # (B, 1, 64, T)
        return self.pool(self.blocks(self.stem(x))).flatten(1)   # (B, 128)

Where do good encoders come from?

  • Supervised pretraining on a big labelled set such as AudioSet (PANNs, YAMNet). Strong, and a good starting point for Module 2's first generation.
  • Self-supervised pretraining on unlabelled audio (below). This is what lets Module 2 adapt to your room's sounds, which AudioSet may describe poorly.
  • Cross-modal pretraining, aligning audio with images or text (for example CLAP for audio–text). A neighbour of what Module 2 does with vision.

Distances and cosine similarity

To decide whether two sounds are "close", we compare their embeddings. The standard choice is cosine similarity, which compares direction and ignores length:

\[ \cos(u, v) = \frac{u \cdot v}{\lVert u\rVert\,\lVert v\rVert} \in [-1, 1]. \]

Length often tracks boring things like loudness, while direction tracks what the sound is. In practice we L2-normalise every embedding to length 1 first. Then cosine similarity is just the dot product, and Euclidean distance becomes a monotone function of it: \(\lVert u - v\rVert^2 = 2 - 2\cos(u, v)\). That is why running k-means (which uses Euclidean distance) on normalised embeddings behaves like clustering by angle.

Self-supervised learning

Self-supervised learning (SSL) invents a training task from the data itself, so no human labels are needed. The task is a pretext; what we want is the encoder it produces. Common pretext tasks for audio:

  • Contrastive: two augmented views of the same clip should have similar embeddings, and views of different clips should not (SimCLR-style, COLA for audio).
  • Masked prediction: hide patches of the spectrogram and predict them (masked autoencoders, BEATs, wav2vec 2.0 for speech).
  • Cross-modal correspondence: does this audio go with this video frame? (Audio-visual correspondence, Arandjelović and Zisserman, 2017.) This is closest to Module 2's spirit.

Contrastive learning and InfoNCE

Take a batch of \(B\) clips. Make two random augmentations of each (different noise, gain, time shift, SpecAugment masks; see Concept 4). Encode both views and normalise, giving \(z_i\) and \(z'_i\). For each clip \(i\), the matching view \(z'_i\) is the positive and the other \(B - 1\) clips' views are negatives. The InfoNCE loss is a softmax classification problem: "which of the \(B\) candidates is my partner?"

\[ \mathcal L = -\frac1B\sum_{i=1}^{B} \log \frac{\exp(z_i \cdot z'_i / \tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{B} \exp(z_i \cdot z'_j / \tau)}. \] 
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def info_nce(z1, z2, temperature=0.1):
    """z1, z2: (B, D) embeddings of two views; row i of each comes from the same clip."""
    z1 = F.normalize(z1, dim=1)
    z2 = F.normalize(z2, dim=1)
    logits = z1 @ z2.T / temperature                # (B, B): every view-1 against every view-2
    targets = torch.arange(z1.shape[0], device=z1.device)   # the diagonal holds the positives
    return F.cross_entropy(logits, targets)
  • The temperature \(\tau\) sharpens the softmax. Smaller values push harder on the most similar negatives; 0.05–0.2 is typical.
  • Bigger batches give more negatives and usually better embeddings. A batch of 256 one-second clips of log-mels fits easily on a Colab or Kaggle GPU.
  • The augmentations decide what the encoder learns to ignore. Noise and gain augmentation teach invariance to the room's hum and to distance, exactly what we want indoors.
  • A subtle trap: two random clips from the same quiet minute are both "fridge hum", yet they are treated as negatives. With unlabelled ambient audio, many negatives are really positives. Larger, more varied batches and dropping near-silent windows both help.

Clustering embeddings

k-means picks \(k\) centroids and assigns each point to the nearest one, alternating until stable. It is fast and simple, and it is the default in Module 2's spec, but know its assumptions: you must choose \(k\), and it assumes round, similarly sized clusters.

from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.preprocessing import normalize

emb = normalize(embeddings)                         # (N, 128), each row unit length
km = KMeans(n_clusters=24, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(emb)
cluster_id = km.labels_                             # (N,) cluster of each window
dist = km.transform(emb).min(axis=1)                # distance to own centroid: a confidence signal
  • Over-cluster. Choose \(k\) larger than the number of classes you expect (say 2–3×). Several clusters can later map to the same label ("microwave hum" and "microwave beep"), and a cluster that is never claimed is just background.
  • Background dominates ambient audio. Most windows are silence, HVAC, or chatter. Remove near-silent windows by energy before clustering, or the clusters will mostly describe the room's hum.
  • Alternatives: HDBSCAN finds clusters of varying density and labels outliers as noise (useful: not every window should get a label); agglomerative clustering lets you cut a tree at different levels.
  • Measuring cluster quality without labels: silhouette score. With a small labelled check set: purity, adjusted Rand index, or normalised mutual information. Hand-label 100–200 windows early; you will need them for every evaluation in Module 2.

Looking at embeddings

You cannot see 128 dimensions, so project them to 2-D with PCA (linear, fast, preserves global layout), t-SNE, or UMAP (non-linear, preserve local neighbourhoods). Colour points by cluster, by time of day, or by the visual tag, and make it clickable so you can listen to a few windows from each cluster. Listening to clusters is the single most useful debugging step in Module 2.

Treat t-SNE and UMAP pictures with care: distances between far-apart groups, and the sizes of groups, mean little. Use them to look, not to measure.

Check your understanding

Q1.Why L2-normalise embeddings before k-means?

So that distance depends only on direction. Unnormalised, loud and quiet versions of the same sound can land far apart just because their embeddings have different lengths. On unit vectors, Euclidean distance and cosine similarity carry the same information.

Q2.In InfoNCE with a batch of 256, how many negatives does each anchor have, and what are the logits' shape?

255 negatives (all other clips' second views). Logits are (256, 256) with positives on the diagonal.

Q3.You run k-means with \(k = 5\) on a day of kitchen audio and all five clusters sound like the fridge. What happened and what do you change?

Background windows vastly outnumber events, so k-means spends all its centroids splitting the background. Drop low-energy windows, raise \(k\) well above the number of expected events, or use a density-based method like HDBSCAN that can mark the background as noise.

Q4.Why do augmentation choices matter so much in contrastive learning?

The encoder learns to make the two views agree, so it learns to ignore whatever the augmentations change. Augment with noise and gain and it becomes noise- and gain-invariant; augment with pitch shift and it may stop distinguishing sounds that differ mainly in pitch.

Common mistakes

  • Clustering raw log-mels instead of embeddings. Distances between raw spectrograms mostly measure loudness and timing.
  • Not removing silence, so clusters describe the background.
  • Choosing \(k\) by eye from a t-SNE plot.
  • No labelled check set, so you cannot tell whether generation 3 is better than generation 1.
  • Mixing embedding spaces: comparing embeddings from different encoder generations or front ends as if they were in the same space. Re-embed everything after each update.
