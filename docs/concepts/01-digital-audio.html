Digital audio: sampling, bit depth, decibels

Every project in this lab starts with a file full of numbers. Before you can build a classifier, a pseudo-labeller, or a localizer, you need to know exactly what those numbers mean: how often they were taken, how precise each one is, how loud they are, and what can silently go wrong when you load them.

The big idea

Sound is a pressure wave: air molecules pushed together and pulled apart, hundreds or thousands of times per second. A microphone turns that pressure into a voltage that rises and falls with it. An analog-to-digital converter (ADC) then measures that voltage at evenly spaced moments and stores each measurement as a number.

So a digital audio clip is just a one-dimensional array \(x[n]\), where \(n\) counts samples. Two numbers tell you how to interpret it:

  • The sample rate \(f_s\): how many measurements per second. 16 kHz means 16,000 numbers per second of audio.
  • The bit depth: how many distinct values each measurement can take. 16-bit audio has 65,536 possible levels.

The sample at index \(n\) happened at time \(t = n / f_s\). A 4-second UrbanSound8K clip at 22,050 Hz is an array of 88,200 numbers. Almost every bug you will meet in audio code comes from forgetting one of these two numbers.

Sampling and the Nyquist limit

A sine wave at frequency \(f\) completes \(f\) cycles per second. To capture it you need at least two samples per cycle, one near each peak and trough. That gives the most important rule in signal processing:

\[ f_{\text{Nyquist}} = \frac{f_s}{2}. \]

A recording at sample rate \(f_s\) can only represent frequencies below \(f_s/2\). At 16 kHz that ceiling is 8 kHz; at 44.1 kHz it is 22.05 kHz, just above the top of human hearing, which is why CDs use that rate.

What happens to frequencies above the limit is worse than "they get lost". They alias: they fold back and show up as a false, lower frequency. A 9 kHz tone sampled at 16 kHz produces exactly the same samples as a 7 kHz tone. Once that happens, no amount of processing can separate them. That is why a real ADC (and a good resampler) applies a low-pass anti-aliasing filter before sampling.

\[ f_{\text{alias}} = \left| f - f_s \cdot \operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{f}{f_s}\right) \right| \]

UrbanSound8K files come at many different sample rates and bit depths. Your data pipeline must resample everything to one rate before computing features, or the same sound will produce different spectrograms depending on which recorder it came from.

Bit depth and quantization

Each sample is rounded to the nearest of \(2^B\) levels, where \(B\) is the bit depth. The rounding error behaves like a tiny bit of added noise, called quantization noise. Each extra bit halves the step size, which lowers that noise by about 6 dB:

\[ \text{SNR}_{\text{quant}} \approx 6.02\,B + 1.76 \ \text{dB}. \]

16-bit audio therefore has about 98 dB of dynamic range, far more than any classifier needs. You will meet this same idea again in Concept 7, where we quantize a network's weights to 8 bits instead of audio samples.

In code, audio usually arrives in one of two forms:

  • Integers (int16, values −32768 to 32767), which is how WAV files usually store it.
  • Floats (float32, scaled to \([-1, 1]\)), which is what torchaudio, librosa, and soundfile return by default. Divide int16 by 32768 to convert.

Decibels

Hearing, and most of audio engineering, works on ratios, not differences. Going from 0.01 to 0.02 sounds like the same step as going from 0.1 to 0.2. The decibel is a logarithmic way to express a ratio:

\[ \text{dB} = 10 \log_{10}\frac{P_1}{P_0} = 20 \log_{10}\frac{A_1}{A_0}. \]

Use \(10\log_{10}\) for power (energy per second, proportional to amplitude squared) and \(20\log_{10}\) for amplitude. They agree because \(\log(A^2) = 2\log A\). Useful anchors:

ChangePower ratioAmplitude ratio
+3 dB×2×1.41
+6 dB×4×2
+10 dB×10×3.16
+20 dB×100×10
−30 dB×0.001×0.032

A decibel value always needs a reference. In digital audio the reference is usually full scale (the largest value the format can hold), written dBFS. A full-scale sine is about −3 dBFS RMS, quiet room noise might be −60 dBFS, and nothing can exceed 0 dBFS without clipping, where the tops of the wave are flattened and distorted.

Two quantities you will compute constantly:

  • RMS level: \(\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{N}\sum x[n]^2}\), the "average loudness" of a clip. Its square is the clip's average power.
  • SNR, signal-to-noise ratio: \(10\log_{10}(P_{\text{signal}}/P_{\text{noise}})\). Module 1 is built around it. Concept 4 shows how to mix noise at an exact SNR.

Channels and file formats

A mono recording has one channel; a stereo one has two; a microphone array has one channel per microphone (4, 6, 8 or more). Libraries disagree about array layout: torchaudio returns (channels, samples), while soundfile returns (samples, channels). Always print the shape.

  • For classification (Modules 1 and 2) we usually average channels down to mono.
  • For localization (Module 3), the differences between channels are the whole signal. Never average them, never resample channels separately, and never apply per-channel gain normalisation before you compute delays.

Formats. WAV and FLAC are lossless; use them for everything in the lab. MP3, AAC, and Opus are lossy: they throw away detail the encoder thinks you won't hear, which can include exactly the high-frequency cues a model uses. If a dataset ships in MP3, decode it once to WAV and keep that.

The code

Loading a clip, making it mono, resampling it to the lab's rate, and measuring its level:

import numpy as np
import torch
import torchaudio

TARGET_SR = 16_000

wav, sr = torchaudio.load("dog_bark.wav")      # wav: (channels, samples), float32 in [-1, 1]
print(wav.shape, sr)                           # e.g. torch.Size([2, 88200]) 22050

wav = wav.mean(dim=0, keepdim=True)            # stereo -> mono: (1, samples)
if sr != TARGET_SR:
    wav = torchaudio.functional.resample(wav, orig_freq=sr, new_freq=TARGET_SR)  # low-pass + resample

def rms_dbfs(x: np.ndarray) -> float:
    """Average level of a float signal in dB relative to full scale."""
    rms = np.sqrt(np.mean(x ** 2))
    return 20 * np.log10(rms + 1e-12)          # epsilon: silence would be log(0)

print(f"{rms_dbfs(wav.numpy()):.1f} dBFS")     # a 0.5-amplitude sine gives about -9.0 dBFS
  • torchaudio.load returns (channels, samples) and the file's own sample rate. Never assume the rate; read it.
  • .mean(dim=0, keepdim=True) averages channels and keeps the channel axis so later code can rely on a 2-D tensor.
  • resample applies an anti-aliasing low-pass filter before changing the rate. Simply taking every other sample (wav[:, ::2]) would alias.
  • 1e-12 keeps log10 finite on silent clips, which do exist in real datasets.

To listen in a notebook: from IPython.display import Audio; Audio(wav.numpy(), rate=TARGET_SR). Listen to your data often. Many pipeline bugs are obvious to the ear and invisible in a loss curve.

Where this shows up in the lab

  • Module 1, /data: every UrbanSound8K clip is resampled, converted to mono, and padded or cropped to a fixed length. Gain drops are specified in dB.
  • Module 1, /eval: robustness is reported as accuracy versus SNR in dB.
  • Module 2, /core/sync: sample indices are converted to timestamps (\(t = n / f_s\)) to line audio up with video frames.
  • Module 3, /dsp/doa: delays between microphones are measured in samples, so the sample rate directly sets the angular resolution.

Check your understanding

Q1.A clip is recorded at 16 kHz. A cricket in it chirps at 9.5 kHz. What appears in the recording?

If the ADC had a proper anti-aliasing filter, nothing: 9.5 kHz is above the 8 kHz Nyquist limit and is removed. Without the filter, it aliases to \(|9.5 - 16| = 6.5\) kHz, a false tone that was never in the room.

Q2.You halve a signal's amplitude. How many dB quieter is it?

\(20\log_{10}(0.5) \approx -6\) dB. Its power drops to a quarter, and \(10\log_{10}(0.25)\) is also about −6 dB.

Q3.A 4-second stereo clip at 48 kHz is loaded with torchaudio. What is the tensor's shape, and what is it after the code above?

Loaded: (2, 192000). After averaging to mono and resampling to 16 kHz: (1, 64000).

Q4.Why must Module 3 never convert its microphone array recording to mono?

Direction is estimated from the tiny time differences between channels. Averaging the channels destroys exactly that information.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming a sample rate instead of reading it from the file, then computing features with the wrong one. Everything is shifted in frequency and nothing errors.
  • Downsampling by slicing (x[::2]) instead of a proper resampler, which aliases high frequencies into the band you keep.
  • Mixing up 10 and 20 in decibel formulas: 10 for power, 20 for amplitude.
  • Mixing int16 and float32 audio, so one source is 32,768 times louder than another.
  • Saving clipped audio: after adding noise or gain, values above 1.0 are clipped when written to 16-bit WAV. Check the peak before saving.
  • Confusing array layouts between torchaudio (C, N) and soundfile (N, C).
