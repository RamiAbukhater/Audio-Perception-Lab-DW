Noise, SNR, reverberation, and augmentation

A model trained on clean, close-mic recordings falls apart in a kitchen with a fan running. This page covers the three ways the real world corrupts sound (additive noise, reverberation, and level changes), how to simulate each one precisely, and how to use those simulations both to train robust models and to measure robustness honestly.

The big idea

What a microphone records, \(y\), is rarely the clean source \(s\). A good first model of reality is

\[ y[n] = g \cdot (s * h)[n] + \alpha\, v[n], \]
  • \(h\) is the room impulse response (RIR), and \(*\) is convolution: the room adds delayed, filtered echoes.
  • \(v\) is additive noise (a fan, traffic, other people), scaled by \(\alpha\) to set the signal-to-noise ratio.
  • \(g\) is a gain: the source is far away, the mic is cheap, or the automatic gain control just ducked.

Each term is a knob. During training we turn the knobs randomly so the model learns to ignore them (data augmentation). During evaluation we turn them to fixed values so we can draw a curve of accuracy versus difficulty (stress testing).

SNR and mixing at a target SNR

The signal-to-noise ratio compares average powers:

\[ \text{SNR}_{\text{dB}} = 10\log_{10}\frac{P_s}{P_v}, \qquad P = \frac{1}{N}\sum_n x[n]^2. \]

To mix noise at an exact target SNR, solve for the scale \(\alpha\) that makes \(P_s / (\alpha^2 P_v) = 10^{\text{SNR}/10}\):

\[ \alpha = \sqrt{\frac{P_s}{P_v \cdot 10^{\text{SNR}/10}}}. \]

Rules of thumb: at 20 dB the noise is clearly audible but the sound is easy to recognise; at 0 dB they are equally strong; at −5 dB the noise is louder than the signal and many models are close to chance.

Mixing at a target SNR

import numpy as np

def mix_at_snr(clean, noise, snr_db, eps=1e-10):
    """Return clean + scaled noise so that the mixture has the requested SNR (in dB)."""
    if len(noise) < len(clean):                       # loop short noise clips
        reps = int(np.ceil(len(clean) / len(noise)))
        noise = np.tile(noise, reps)
    start = np.random.randint(0, len(noise) - len(clean) + 1)
    noise = noise[start:start + len(clean)]            # random crop, same length as clean

    p_clean = np.mean(clean ** 2)
    p_noise = np.mean(noise ** 2)
    scale = np.sqrt(p_clean / (p_noise * 10 ** (snr_db / 10) + eps))
    mixture = clean + scale * noise

    peak = np.max(np.abs(mixture))
    if peak > 1.0:                                     # avoid clipping; scales signal and noise together,
        mixture = mixture / peak                       # so the SNR is unchanged
    return mixture.astype(np.float32)
  • Powers are averages (np.mean), so the result does not depend on clip length.
  • The final rescale multiplies signal and noise by the same factor, so it changes loudness but not SNR.
  • Silent clips have \(P_s \approx 0\) and get almost no noise. UrbanSound8K has clips where the event fills only part of the 4 seconds; computing \(P_s\) over the whole clip then understates the event's power. For careful stress tests, compute \(P_s\) over the frames where the event is active.

White, pink, and real noise

  • White noise has equal power at every frequency. It sounds like hiss and is easy to generate (np.random.randn). It is a good model of sensor and electronic noise.
  • Pink noise has power falling as \(1/f\): equal power per octave. It sounds like a waterfall or steady rain and is much closer to most environmental noise. Because its energy sits at low frequencies, it masks more of the information in a mel spectrogram than white noise at the same SNR. Expect pink noise to hurt more.
  • Real noise recordings (fans, cafés, traffic, HVAC) are the most realistic. Good sources: DNS Challenge noise, MUSAN, and DEMAND. Keep the noise files used for training separate from those used for testing.
def pink_noise(n, rng=np.random.default_rng()):
    """Unit-variance pink (1/f power) noise via spectral shaping of white noise."""
    white = rng.standard_normal(n)
    spectrum = np.fft.rfft(white)
    freqs = np.fft.rfftfreq(n)
    freqs[0] = freqs[1]                     # avoid dividing by zero at DC
    spectrum = spectrum / np.sqrt(freqs)    # amplitude ~ 1/sqrt(f)  =>  power ~ 1/f
    pink = np.fft.irfft(spectrum, n)
    return (pink / np.std(pink)).astype(np.float32)

Reverberation and room impulse responses

Clap in a stairwell and you hear the clap, then a dense tail of echoes fading away. The room impulse response \(h\) is exactly that recording: what a microphone picks up when the source emits a perfect, instantaneous click. It has three parts:

  1. the direct path, the first and usually strongest peak;
  2. early reflections, distinct echoes off the nearest walls, floor, and ceiling in the first ~50 ms;
  3. the late reverberant tail, so many overlapping reflections that it looks like decaying noise.

Because a room is (approximately) a linear, time-invariant system, the reverberant version of any sound is the convolution of the dry sound with the RIR. The RT60 is the time it takes the tail to decay by 60 dB: about 0.3 s in a furnished bedroom, 0.6–1 s in a classroom, 2 s or more in a church.

from scipy.signal import fftconvolve

def add_reverb(clean, rir):
    """Convolve with a room impulse response, keep the original length and loudness."""
    rir = rir / np.max(np.abs(rir))
    direct = np.argmax(np.abs(rir))                       # index of the direct-path peak
    wet = fftconvolve(clean, rir)[direct:direct + len(clean)]   # drop the propagation delay
    wet *= np.sqrt(np.mean(clean ** 2) / (np.mean(wet ** 2) + 1e-10))
    return wet.astype(np.float32)
  • fftconvolve is much faster than np.convolve for RIRs, which are thousands of samples long.
  • Cropping from the direct-path index keeps the event aligned with its label in time. Without it, every reverberant clip is shifted by the source-to-mic travel time.
  • Matching RMS stops reverb from also acting as a hidden gain change, so the two effects stay separate in your experiments.
  • Where to get RIRs: measured sets such as the OpenSLR 28 RIR and noise database, or simulate them with pyroomacoustics (Concept 12). Resample RIRs to the same rate as your audio.

Gain drops

The spec calls for dynamic gain drops: the level changes during a clip, as when a source moves away, a hand covers the mic, or automatic gain control kicks in. Simulate with a random gain in dB, either for the whole clip or as a smooth envelope that dips for part of it:

def random_gain(x, rng=np.random.default_rng(), low_db=-30, high_db=0):
    g_db = rng.uniform(low_db, high_db)
    return x * 10 ** (g_db / 20)            # amplitude, so divide by 20

Apply gain before adding noise if you want to model a quieter source in the same noisy room (SNR falls), and after if you want to model the whole recording getting quieter (SNR unchanged). Those are different experiments; say which one you ran.

SpecAugment

Some augmentation happens on the spectrogram rather than the waveform. SpecAugment (Park et al., 2019) zeroes out random bands of frequencies and random stretches of time, forcing the model not to depend on any single region. It is cheap and almost always helps.

import torchaudio.transforms as T

spec_aug = torch.nn.Sequential(
    T.FrequencyMasking(freq_mask_param=8),   # mask up to 8 of the 64 mel bands
    T.TimeMasking(time_mask_param=20),       # mask up to 20 frames (400 ms)
)
x_train = spec_aug(logmel)                   # training only, never at evaluation

Other useful augmentations: random time shift, mixup (blend two clips and their labels), and small pitch or speed changes. Be careful with pitch and speed: a pitch-shifted siren is still a siren, but a pitch-shifted engine may sound like a different engine.

Augmenting versus stress-testing

Keep these two uses separate

Training augmentation is random, applied on the fly, and uses the training noise and RIR sets. Stress testing uses fixed SNR levels (for example −5, 0, 5, 10, 20 dB and clean), a fixed random seed, and a held-out set of noises and RIRs the model never saw. If the same fan recording appears in both, your robustness curve is measuring memorisation.

Where this shows up in the lab

  • Module 1, /data: pink and white noise injection, RIR reverberation, and gain drops, exactly as above. /eval sweeps SNR to draw accuracy-degradation curves.
  • Module 2: the same augmentations create the two "views" of a clip used in contrastive learning (Concept 8), and help the student model generalise beyond its pseudo-labels.
  • Module 3: reverberation is the enemy of localization. Strong reflections create false correlation peaks, which is why GCC-PHAT and the physics layer exist.

Check your understanding

Q1.Signal power is 0.01 and noise power is 0.04 after scaling. What is the SNR?

\(10\log_{10}(0.01/0.04) = 10\log_{10}(0.25) \approx -6\) dB. The noise is four times stronger than the signal.

Q2.At the same SNR, why does pink noise usually hurt a log-mel classifier more than white noise?

Pink noise puts most of its power at low frequencies, where mel bands are narrow and numerous and where most class information lives. White noise spreads its power evenly in Hz, so most of it lands in the few wide high-frequency mel bands.

Q3.Why crop the convolved signal starting at the RIR's direct-path peak?

Measured RIRs start with silence while the sound travels from source to mic. Without cropping, every reverberant clip is delayed by that amount, which misaligns events with their timestamps and wastes part of the fixed-length window.

Q4.You train with noise from MUSAN and test robustness with noise from MUSAN. What is wrong?

Nothing, if the specific files are split into disjoint train and test sets. If the same recordings appear in both, the test measures how well the model ignores noises it has memorised, not how robust it is to new ones.

Common mistakes

  • Using sums instead of means for power, so SNR depends on clip length.
  • Using 20 instead of 10 in the SNR scale formula (or the reverse), giving SNRs off by a factor of two in dB.
  • Augmenting the test set with random augmentation, so every evaluation run gives a different number.
  • Normalising each mixture to the same peak and then reporting "gain drop" experiments, which undoes the gain.
  • RIRs at the wrong sample rate: a 48 kHz RIR applied to 16 kHz audio makes the room three times bigger.
  • Forgetting to listen. Play ten augmented examples before training. You will catch clipping, silence, and misalignment immediately.
