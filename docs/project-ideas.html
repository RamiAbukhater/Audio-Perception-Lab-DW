Project ideas

The Multi-Modal Audio Perception Lab runs one research project: teaching an audio model the sounds that pretrained models get wrong, using a camera instead of a person. Off-the-shelf audio models such as CLAP already recognise common sounds like "microwave" or "typing". They cannot tell which microwave beeped, they have no word for many room-specific sounds, and they degrade on far-field audio from a fixed room microphone. In this project a fixed camera names the sounds, and a spatial gate built from a microphone array and a 3-D room map checks which visible object actually made each one before its label is trusted.

Hearing what pretrained models can't: spatially verified pseudo-labels from a camera

Self-supervisedOpen-vocabulary detectorMic array · GCC-PHATRGB-D · Open3DCLAP baseline
Closest prior work
  • Wu, Harrison, Bigham & Laput (2020). Automated Class Discovery and One-Shot Interactions for Acoustic Activity Recognition (Listen Learner). Already does: discovers a home's own sound classes by clustering audio embeddings on a 4-mic device, then asks a person once per class ("what was that sound?"). Does not: use a camera; every class still needs a human answer.
  • Patidar, Goel & Agarwal (2023). VAX: Using Existing Video and Audio-based Activity Recognition Models to Bootstrap Privacy-Sensitive Sensors. Already does: pretrained video models and YAMNet label activities in 10 homes, and those labels train other sensors, including a microphone array's sound direction. Does not: use direction to check whether a label is right, or learn anything the pretrained models cannot already name (about 2 of 17 activities still needed a person's label); the model it trains is not an audio classifier.
  • Jain et al. (2022). ProtoSound: A Personalized and Scalable Sound Recognition System for Deaf and Hard-of-Hearing Users. Already does: fine-grained, home-specific sound classes from a few user recordings. Does not: use a camera or spatial checks; a person records every class.
  • Vasudevan, Dai & Van Gool (2020), Gan et al. (2019), and Valverde et al. (2021). Already do: a vision teacher trains binaural, stereo, or array audio to predict what is where. Do not: verify the teacher's labels, or handle walls, echoes, and occlusion; they work outdoors on vehicles and street scenes.
  • Yang et al. (2025). Clink! Chop! Thud! Learning Object Sounds from Real-World Interactions. Already does: picks which candidate object is making a sound in egocentric video. Does not: use a microphone array or room physics; the choice is learned from appearance.
  • An, Son, Manocha & Yoon (2017). Reflection-Aware Sound Source Localization. Already does: a 3-D map from RGB-D, echo tracing, and localization without line of sight. Does not: recognise anything or feed into learning.

What is still open: replacing Listen Learner's human question with a camera, so new and room-specific sound classes get names with zero human labels, and using a spatial gate (direction of arrival checked against a 3-D room map, including line of sight and echoes) to decide which visible object made each sound before its label is trusted. VAX records sound direction but never uses it to accept or reject a label. We found no paper that combines these, from about 16 targeted searches; read Listen Learner and VAX in full before writing a proposal.

Objective. In a fixed indoor space, learn to recognise sounds that pretrained audio models such as CLAP get wrong or cannot name (specific instances, room-specific events, and sounds outside AudioSet's 527 classes), with no human sound labels. A fixed camera supplies the names; a spatial gate verifies that each named object really produced the sound; an audio model trained only on the room's own recordings learns from the verified labels, and is compared directly against CLAP and the other baselines below.

How the system works.
  1. Synchronised capture: a static microphone array and an RGB-D camera record a shared indoor space, every sample and frame stamped on one clock.
  2. Visual naming: an open-vocabulary detector (for example YOLO-World, OWL-ViT, or Grounding DINO) finds and names objects from a text vocabulary far larger than COCO's 80 classes, object tracking gives each one a persistent identity ("microwave #1", "microwave #2"), and depth gives its 3-D position.
  3. In-room audio clusters: an audio encoder trained only on this room's recordings (self-supervised, Concept 7) embeds each window, and clustering groups windows that sound alike. Pretrained audio models are kept out of the labelling path, so the comparison with them is fair.
  4. Co-occurrence proposals: a cluster that appears far more often than chance with an object, or with an action on it (a door opening, a hand at the kettle), proposes that object as the label.
  5. Spatial gate, the core contribution:
    • Direction check: GCC-PHAT or SRP-PHAT on the array gives the sound's azimuth and elevation, which must point at the proposed object, and at that instance rather than another of the same class.
    • Line-of-sight check: ray casting against the room map (point cloud, walls, doorways) confirms nothing blocks the path between array and object.
    • Echo attribution: when the strongest arrival comes from a wall, mirror-source geometry tests whether it is an echo of the proposed object; if so, the label is kept and credited to that object.
    • Rejection: if the sound comes from another object, from a wall with no matching mirror image, or from off camera, the label is refused and the window is kept for the out-of-view test.
  6. Iterative self-training: the student audio model trains on the gated labels with strong augmentation, relabels the unlabelled windows, and the loop repeats, with the gate applied every generation.

Suggested path.
  1. Find the gap first. Record a few days of audio in the target room and run CLAP zero-shot (and an open-vocabulary detector such as Detect Any Sound) on it. List the sounds it gets wrong, confuses between instances, or has no class for. That list is the project's target set; if it is empty, pick a different room.
  2. Simulation. In SoundSpaces 2.0 (or pyroomacoustics for audio only), place sounding objects, including two of the same kind, render audio at a simulated array plus RGB-D views, and measure how often the spatial gate picks the right instance in visible, occluded, off-screen, and distractor cases.
  3. Capture rig and consent. A synchronised array (for example a ReSpeaker 4- or 6-mic array) and an RGB-D camera (for example a RealSense) on one machine and clock; calibrate array-to-camera with a phone playing noise from known spots; build the room map once. Get consent from everyone who uses the space.
  4. Check set. Hand-label 200+ windows from held-out days, covering both common sounds and the target set, so every method is scored on the same data.
  5. Generation 0 and iteration. Cluster, propose labels by co-occurrence, gate them, train the student, relabel, repeat. Track pseudo-label precision and coverage, test macro-F1, and how many labels each spatial check rejected, per generation, with the gate on and off.
  6. Compare and probe. Score the student against every baseline below on the target set, on common sounds, and on sounds whose source was out of view (the gate's rejected windows are a ready-made test set).
Key result. A table of macro-F1 on the target set (instance-level, room-specific, and out-of-ontology sounds) for CLAP zero-shot, an open-vocabulary detector, camera labels without the gate, camera labels with the gate, and a Listen Learner-style one-label-per-class human upper bound, plus precision-over-generations curves with the gate on and off. The research question: can a camera, checked by room physics, teach an audio model the sounds that pretrained models miss, with no human labels, and come close to a system that asks a person?
Extensions.
  • A soft spatial confidence (likelihood from angular error and arrival order) multiplied into the pseudo-label score, instead of a yes/no gate.
  • Hybrid: fall back to one Listen Learner-style human question only for clusters the camera cannot name, and measure how many questions that saves.
  • Learned direction of arrival trained on the gate's accepted labels, so the spatial side improves each generation too.
  • Remove the camera after training and keep only the audio model, as VAX does for its sensors, for a privacy-preserving deployment.
  • Transfer: train in one room, test in another with the same kinds of objects.

Needs: Python, CLAP, pyroomacoustics, and SoundSpaces for the first phases; a synchronised microphone array, an RGB-D camera, and a GPU for the real-room phase; a shared indoor space where everyone has agreed to be recorded.

What is new, and what is not

Each ingredient exists. Listen Learner discovers a home's own sound classes but asks a person to name each one. VAX uses a camera and pretrained models to bootstrap a model in each home, and even records sound direction, but never uses direction to check a label, and cannot learn what its pretrained models cannot name. Vision teaching spatial audio has been done outdoors, and room-aware localization has been done without recognition. A reviewer will read this project as "Listen Learner plus VAX plus room-aware localization", so the claim has to be specific:

  1. The camera replaces the human question, turning one question per class into zero.
  2. The spatial gate decides which object to credit, including which of two identical objects and whether a visible object is a silent bystander. No paper above does this.
  3. The targets are the sounds pretrained models miss, and the comparison against CLAP is made on exactly those.

One honest limit: a camera can only name what an open-vocabulary detector can name. The project moves the vocabulary from an audio ontology to a much larger visual one, plus object identity from tracking. A sound whose source is never visible, or that no detector can describe, is still out of reach without a person.
